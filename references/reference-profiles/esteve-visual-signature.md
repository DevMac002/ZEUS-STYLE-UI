# Esteve visual signature

A personal visual starting point distilled from the screenshots supplied by the user: a minimal folding-bike product showcase and a bright coastal/kiteboarding experience. These are visual references, not instructions to reproduce either site.

## Shared character

The two examples share a clean, spacious presentation that lets the subject lead. Preserve these qualities across different themes:

- A light canvas, often white or warm white, with generous negative space.
- One strong subject image or product visualization, large enough to carry the composition.
- Editorial asymmetry instead of a uniform grid of cards.
- Layered UI panels, product cut-outs, or a large graphic shape that relates to the content.
- Clear, compact navigation and restrained supporting labels.
- Modern sans-serif typography; condensed uppercase display type can add an editorial accent.
- Small but confident color moments and polished transitions that reveal product details.

## Default blend

Use the minimal product showcase as the baseline: quiet navigation, generous open space, and one large subject. Borrow a fluid form or overlapping panel from the lifestyle reference only when it reinforces the project theme. Keep the result light and uncluttered. Choose colors from the actual brand and subject; the reference colors are not required.

## Two useful expressions

### Minimal product showcase

Use when the product itself is the story: bicycles, devices, furniture, fashion, or other designed objects.

- Keep the background light and the header quiet.
- Give the product scale and room; a horizontal gallery or a product image that crosses a panel can create movement.
- Use a deep navy or indigo panel for selected product details or configuration, with a small coral action accent if it suits the brand.
- Keep price, options, and primary action easy to find. Interactions such as selecting a product or configuring it must be real and clear.

### Airy lifestyle and activity

Use when a place, activity, or experience is part of the story: travel, sport, wellness, coastal products, or hospitality.

- Keep the setting bright and tactile. Let a photograph or product image interact with the interface.
- Aqua or turquoise wave-like forms can suggest water or motion; a cobalt or indigo panel can hold useful information; coral can spotlight the featured object or action.
- Overlap a few panels with the scene, keeping text legible and the reading order obvious.
- Use motion to reveal the product, activity, or a meaningful data point, not as a continuous decoration.

These colors and forms are examples observed in the supplied references. They are not mandatory for every matching project; derive the palette and motif from each project's actual theme and brand.

## Composition and interaction

- Build the first screen around one memorable subject and one clear next action.
- Balance visual richness with open space; do not fill every quiet area.
- Let overlap create depth, but keep content hierarchy, keyboard order, and small-screen layout straightforward.
- Prefer a few distinctive panels to repeated generic cards.
- On mobile, change the composition deliberately: stack or sequence image and information, preserve the primary action, and avoid shrinking a desktop overlap until it becomes cramped.
- Use meaningful hover, selection, transition, or scroll states. Respect reduced-motion preferences.
- Keep product controls, navigation, and calls to action functional; visual prototypes must not imply unavailable features.

## Staying on theme

Creativity should begin with the project's subject, audience, brand, and intended feeling. Transfer the references' principles—space, scale, contrast, layering, and reveal—rather than their exact colors, copy, imagery, or layout. If the project's identity points elsewhere, preserve that identity and use only the principles that still fit.
